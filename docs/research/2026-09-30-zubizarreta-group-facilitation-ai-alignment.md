## Rosa's claims: what could change the OFL questions

- **Facilitation as epistemic scaffolding.** Her proposed shift is from “argument as battle” toward co-creation: the group may become better able to explore, challenge and shape ideas together. She describes facilitation as an embodied intervention in how a group knows, not merely timekeeping or turn-taking. **OFL implication:** ask practitioners whether a change in the group's way of working together is a desirable outcome, what observable signs would distinguish it from polished language or forced agreement, and which methods would actually claim it. This is a candidate question, not an accepted `layer: outcome` criterion or an observed effect.
- **Human alignment cannot be delegated to AI.** Rosa argues that alignment of social institutions with humane values and the growth of human facilitative capacity are human work. AI may support learning but cannot perform that growth on our behalf. **OFL implication:** distinguish a faithful run, a helpful session, and a longer-term change in people's ability to deliberate or facilitate *without* the tool. The last requires longitudinal or unassisted evidence, not a single transcript or an AI's self-description. This connects to the open capacity question in `evals#3`, without treating that measurement design as a settled criterion.
- **Embodied care work is easy to render invisible.** Rosa says facilitation's emotional and relational labour is often gendered and undervalued. **OFL implication:** ask practitioners what a session report misses about whether people could voice a concern, remain in tension, and own what emerged. Do not convert this into a universal, automatically scored empathy metric or claim that a transcript captures everything that mattered.
- **AI as a tutor for human empathy.** Rosa suggests that LLMs' perceived empathy might help people practise empathic responses. She does not report a tested training programme. **OFL implication:** if explored, evaluate human practice and later unassisted capacity, not just whether the tutor produces a plausible response. Treat tutoring humans and facilitating a group as different intervention roles.

## Boundary with existing OFL artifacts

`docs/conformance/architecture.md` assigns outcome evaluation to the practice, not the product; the layer currently has no agreed criteria. A session's **desired outcome** (for example, people can explore a disagreement together and own what they produced) is not the same as a **method-essential action** (for example, reflect a contribution onto the correct board), an **interaction-profile rule**, or an **absence-of-harm detector** such as premature closure. Practitioners must decide whether co-creation is relevant to a given method and how to recognise it without rewarding conformity or artificial consensus.

`methods/dynamic-facilitation/method.md` already describes the four boards, reflecting contributions, holding conflict, and group-owned choice-creating. Its stage evals test facilitator behaviour. Rosa's essay does not independently validate those checks, establish that they were used in a particular Austrian assembly, or show that the group experienced a beneficial outcome. Do not duplicate a four-board method from the AI conversation as a new discovery.

## AI-generated proposals in the published dialogue: leads, not sourced findings

The Google AI responses describe named Austrian organisers, staffing, selection and outcomes; attribute Art of Hosting, Sociocracy, fishbowls, and Dynamic Facilitation to particular assemblies; and propose dynamic harvesting, objection integration, and an empathy-tutoring loop. Those specifics were **generated by the AI**, and Rosa says she removed its references for readability. Her own response says dynamic harvesting and objection integration *may* connect with the organisers' Dynamic Facilitation experience; she does not certify the AI's claims. Verify each historical or method-attribution claim against an independent primary source and method steward before putting it into a spec, criterion or public presentation.

The tutor loop is nonetheless a testable *design proposal*: a person attempts to reflect a difficult statement, receives feedback on what they missed, retries, and is later assessed without AI assistance. Whether it builds empathy or transfers to real group facilitation remains untested here. Tom Atlee's supportive comment supplies no further mechanism.

## Next questions for practitioners, not instructions to implement

- After a session, could participants explain a difference they now understand without having to agree with it? What would show they generated or revised possibilities together, rather than repeated an AI synthesis?
- What evidence would show the group owns the result, and which important shifts would a transcript alone miss?
- What would count as improvement in unassisted listening or deliberation later, and what would count as dependence on AI?
- Which methods seek co-creation, which aim at another outcome, and who has standing to decide?
